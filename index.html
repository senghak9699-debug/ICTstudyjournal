SMT — Smart Money Technique Divergence

A non-confirmation between two correlated instruments at matching swing points. When one asset takes out a prior high/low and the correlated one fails to, that "crack in correlation" exposes hidden strength or weakness before price alone shows it.

How to Identify It

  • Pick a genuinely correlated pair: NQ↔ES, EURUSD↔GBPUSD, or your own Gold vs Silver setup.
  • Watch both charts swing-by-swing during the same session/killzone.
  • Bullish SMT: one asset prints a lower low, the other holds a higher low.
  • Bearish SMT: one asset prints a higher high, the other fails with a lower high.
  • Weight it higher when it lines up with a liquidity sweep and a HTF PD array (order block/FVG) — naked SMT with no location is low conviction.
  • Never fight a strong HTF trend with a low-timeframe SMT reading — it's a scalp signal at best in that case.

Judas Swing

A false move early in a session — a push in one direction, usually right after a session open, that runs resting liquidity before the day's real move develops the other way. It's the "manipulation" leg of Power of Three.

How to Identify It

  • Anchor to the New York midnight open (00:00 EST) or the London/NY session open.
  • Watch for a push beyond the Asian range high or low, or beyond the midnight opening price, in the 00:00–05:00 EST window.
  • Confirm it's a Judas Swing (not the real move) by requiring a Market Structure Shift (MSS) back through the opening price shortly after the fake move.
  • The false move must actually run known liquidity (session high/low, Asian range extreme) — a random wiggle isn't a Judas Swing.
  • Once MSS confirms the reversal, look for an FVG or Order Block on the true-direction side for entry.

Silver Bullet

A time-based scalping model targeting one-hour windows — 3:00–4:00 AM, 10:00–11:00 AM, and 2:00–3:00 PM EST — looking for a liquidity sweep followed by a retracement into a Fair Value Gap for entry.

How to Identify It

  • Only look for it inside the three fixed windows above — outside them, it isn't a valid Silver Bullet setup.
  • Establish bias from the 15M timeframe liquidity picture first.
  • Drop to the 1M–3M chart and wait for a Market Structure Shift (MSS) confirming direction.
  • Locate the FVG left behind by the displacement that caused the MSS.
  • Enter on the retrace into that FVG — top of the gap for longs, bottom for shorts.
  • Stop loss beyond the FVG-forming candle's high/low; target opposing liquidity or the next imbalance.

OTE — Optimal Trade Entry

The 61.8%–79% Fibonacci retracement band of an impulse leg, with 70.5% marked as the sweet spot. It operationalizes premium/discount: entries are taken deep into the pullback, close to invalidation, before the leg's continuation resumes.

How to Identify It

  • Pick a clean impulse leg — a displacement that broke structure, with an unambiguous swing low and swing high. A choppy/overlapping move doesn't qualify.
  • Anchor the Fibonacci retracement: swing low → swing high for longs, swing high → swing low for shorts.
  • Mark the 0.62–0.79 band (0.705 as midline) — that band, not one ratio, is the OTE zone.
  • Require confluence: an Order Block or FVG sitting inside the OTE band significantly raises conviction.
  • OTE is not a standalone signal — the sequence is: liquidity taken → structure shifted → retracement into OTE + a PD array.

BPR — Balanced Price Range

The overlap of two opposing Fair Value Gaps. Price displaces one way and leaves a gap, then displaces back through the same area, leaving an opposing gap. The overlapping span has now been "delivered" both ways — hence balanced.

How to Identify It

  • Locate a bullish FVG and a bearish FVG that sit directly on top of each other (horizontally overlapping).
  • The more recent gap sets the expected bias for the zone: a bullish FVG overrun by a newer bearish FVG → watched as resistance; the mirror sequence → watched as support.
  • BPRs are high-sensitivity zones — price often reacts fast and sharply when it returns to them, since two opposing imbalances stack in one place.
  • Check whether the BPR sits in a discount (for longs) or premium (for shorts) zone of the current dealing range for added confluence.
  • Distinguish from a simple Inversion FVG: an iFVG is one gap that failed and flipped role; a BPR is two gaps that both delivered, overlapping.

MMXM — Market Maker Model

The blueprint for how price moves from one PD array to the opposite one: original consolidation → price run → smart money reversal → accumulation/distribution → completion. Two states: MMBM (Market Maker Buy Model) and MMSM (Market Maker Sell Model).

How to Identify It

  • Start from HTF PD array context — is price coming off a premium or discount array?
  • Look for the "original consolidation" — a tight range where the reversal will originate.
  • Watch for SMT divergence between correlated assets as an early warning of the reversal.
  • Confirm with a liquidity sweep of the consolidation's high/low (engineered liquidity) followed by displacement the opposite way.
  • MMBM: bearish trend → SMT/sweep → displacement up → accumulation → markup.
  • MMSM: bullish trend → SMT/sweep → displacement down → distribution → markdown.
  • Always frame the model relative to the bigger timeframe's external range — MMXM tells you the *path*, not just a single reversal point.

Power of Three (PO3 / AMD)

A reading of how any trading period unfolds in three phases: Accumulation (range building), Manipulation (liquidity sweep / Judas Swing), Distribution (the genuine directional move, often opposite to the manipulation leg).

How to Identify It

  • Accumulation: price builds a tight opening range (Asian range, daily open range) — direction unknown, don't trade inside it.
  • Manipulation: price breaks that range — treat this as a warning, not an entry signal; the theory says this exists to trap the eager side.
  • Distribution: confirmed once the manipulation move is reclaimed/reversed — this is where the model believes the real move begins.
  • Apply the same AMD lens at multiple nested scales: daily PO3, session PO3, and even a single killzone's own mini-PO3.
  • Combine with Judas Swing (manipulation = the Judas leg) and MSS (confirms the distribution phase has started).

Advanced PD Arrays

ICT's collective name for the reference points a market leaves behind: old highs/lows, order blocks, FVGs, liquidity voids, breaker blocks, mitigation blocks, rejection blocks. Split the dealing range at equilibrium (50%) — every array sits in the premium or discount half.

How to Identify It

  • Mark the dealing range (swing high to swing low) and its 50% equilibrium first — everything else is graded relative to that line.
  • Catalogue every array inside each half: unmitigated order blocks, open FVGs, breaker blocks, mitigation blocks, old highs/lows, rejection blocks.
  • Rank arrays by strength: has it been tested before (weaker) or is it fresh/unmitigated (stronger)?
  • Use the "traded-to-most-recently" logic: if discount arrays just provided support, expect price to reach for premium arrays next, and vice versa.
  • Use HTF arrays to define the zone worth trading, then let LTF arrays inside that zone refine your actual entry.

Intermarket Analysis

The idea that a move in one market has a cause-and-effect ripple into correlated markets. For Gold: DXY and US Treasury yields are the two primary reference instruments — both typically inverse to Gold.

How to Identify It

  • Before any XAUUSD bias call, check DXY direction — a falling dollar is tailwind for Gold, a rising dollar is headwind.
  • Check the relevant US Treasury yield (10Y is the common reference) — rising yields typically pressure Gold since it pays no interest; falling yields tend to support it.
  • Look for alignment across all three (Gold, DXY, yields) before treating a setup as high-conviction; disagreement between them is a reason to lower conviction or wait.
  • Extend the same logic to bond-vs-equity and commodity-index relationships when relevant to broader risk-on/risk-off context.
  • Use intermarket read as context/filter, not as a standalone entry signal — it confirms or denies the story your ICT structural analysis is already telling you.

Time & Price

A core ICT tenet: time is more important than price. The Interbank Price Delivery Algorithm (IPDA) is theorized to hunt liquidity at specific times of day — killzones — rather than at random. A pattern arriving at the "wrong" time carries far less weight than a liquidity grab arriving inside a killzone.

How to Identify It

  • Always anchor analysis to New York time, regardless of your local session.
  • Know the standard killzones: Asian (~8–10 PM), London Open (~2–5 AM), New York AM (~7–10 AM), London Close (~10 AM–12 PM), New York PM (~1:30–4 PM) — treat edges as soft, not exact.
  • Rate any setup partly by *when* it occurs: a textbook pattern outside a killzone gets discounted; a simple liquidity grab inside one gets weighted up.
  • Avoid the New York lunch/dead zone (~11:30 AM–1 PM) — low-probability, reversal-trap territory.
  • Layer day-of-week and monthly/quarterly cyclical tendencies on top of killzone timing for additional context (per your project's existing Time & Price checklist item).
